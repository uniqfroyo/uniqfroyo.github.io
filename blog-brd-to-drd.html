How to Convert BRD to DRD

A requirement framework for turning a vague business problem into an executable design spec — one translation step at a time.

At its core, this framework is a pipeline for translation. It takes a business problem that starts out completely unstructured, and translates it — layer by layer — into something an engineer or designer can actually build. The value isn't in memorizing the stage names; it's in understanding what problem each layer solves, and a few ideas that run underneath the whole thing.

The pipeline: from problem to spec

It starts with a business scenario — an unstructured description of what happened and why it needs solving. Nothing here is structured yet; this is pure problem-sensing.

The first real structuring happens at the business use case layer: the scenario gets broken into how the business actually operates — who does what, under what rules, with what inputs and outputs. This is business logic on its own terms, with no product or interface attached yet. From there, a user task grounds that logic in a specific person's concrete actions — the smallest unit of behavior, like each click in an approval flow.

The real pivot is the product use case: the user task gets re-framed inside the product itself — as task flows, functional modules, automation rules, permissions, and the UI for every empty, loading, and error state. This is the moment business logic actually becomes product language. From there, a PRD formalizes what the product must do and how well it must do it, and a DRD turns that PRD into an executable design spec — goals, research, information architecture, interaction detail, visuals, accessibility.

A quality gate sits at each handoff point, forcing validation before anything moves forward — so a flaw doesn't get carried down the whole pipeline. And none of this runs once and stops: requirement iteration is a loop — start from insight, not from requirements; turn the insight into a problem statement; remodel it through user stories and tasks; validate the hypothesis through experiments; update the PRD, the design, and the technical approach; then read the post-launch data back into the next cycle. That ordering matters — it's a correction to the common habit of starting with "what feature should we build" instead of "what did we just observe, and what does it mean."

Three ideas worth taking away

The layered framework itself is fairly common. What's actually useful in this document are three sharper judgments underneath it.

1. Business use case vs. product use case is a translation from a business problem into an experience problem

A business use case lives at the logical layer — measured by process efficiency, cost, and risk. A product use case lives at the experience layer — measured by completion rate, usability, and error rate. Abstracting the former into the latter is, in effect, translating a business problem into an experience problem. That's a fairly precise definition of what product design work actually is: not drawing screens, but translation.

2. Design systems and business requirements run on different timescales, and need explicit rules to coexist

A design system optimizes for long-term consistency and reuse across products. Business requirements optimize for one product's immediate goals. Left alone, these two pull against each other — every team wants an exception. The governance logic here is clear: business requirements can push the design system to expand; the design system gives business requirements "guardrails"; and business requirements take priority, but can't break the system's rules. That's the difference between a design system that actually holds up and one that quietly gets ignored the first time a deadline is tight.

3. Requirements themselves can be abstracted into reusable patterns — not just UI components

This is the point most easily missed. If a business use case is treated as a self-contained unit of work — with its own inputs, outputs, and stored data — it functions like an independent micro-system. The requirement knowledge built for handling it can then be abstracted into a reusable pattern for similar problems later, instead of writing every spec from scratch. In other words: design capability isn't just finding patterns in interfaces — it's finding patterns in the requirements process itself, and building a system out of them.
