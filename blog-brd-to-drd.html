How to Convert BRD to DRD

A framework for turning a loosely defined business problem into a design specification that a team can implement.

A business requirements document (BRD) can explain what the business needs while leaving open how the product should respond. This framework bridges that gap. It shows what each stage needs to clarify before a team can make design and implementation decisions.

From business problem to design specification

Start with a business scenario. Describe what happened, who was affected, and why the problem needs attention.

A business use case describes how the business handles that situation: who does the work, what rules apply, what information goes in, and what comes out. A user task then focuses on what one person needs to do, such as reviewing a request before approving it.

A product use case describes how the product supports that task. It covers task flows, features, automation, permissions, and empty, loading, and error states. The product requirements document (PRD) records what the product must do and how well it must do it. The DRD turns those requirements into a design specification covering goals, research findings, information architecture, interactions, visual design, and accessibility.

Use a quality gate at each handoff to check that the work is ready for the next stage. When you uncover a new insight, turn it into a problem statement, describe who is affected and what they need to do, then test your assumptions. Use the results to update the PRD, design, and technical approach. After launch, use product data to inform the next round of decisions.

Three ideas worth taking away

1. Business and product use cases answer different questions

A business use case asks how well a process works, using measures such as efficiency, cost, and risk. A product use case asks how well people can complete the same work in the product, using measures such as task completion, usability, and error rates. Product design connects the two by turning a business need into an experience people can use.

2. Design systems and business requirements work on different timescales

A design system supports consistency and reuse across products over time. Business requirements address a product’s immediate goals. When an existing pattern falls short, the team can extend the system while preserving its shared rules. Clear governance helps teams make that decision even under deadline pressure.

3. Requirements can become reusable patterns

Treat a business use case as a unit of work with defined inputs, outputs, and data that needs to be stored. Documenting those requirements gives the next team a starting point when it encounters a similar problem. That makes it possible to reuse the thinking behind an interface as well as its components.
